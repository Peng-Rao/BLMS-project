\section{Data and Problem}
\label{sec:intro}

We analyse the \emph{Yacht Hydrodynamics} dataset from the UCI Machine Learning
Repository \autocite{ortigosa2007yacht,gerritsma1981delft}. The data come from
towing-tank experiments on the Delft systematic yacht hull series. Each
observation corresponds to one hull form tested at one speed. The
response variable is the residuary resistance per unit weight of displacement,
while the predictors describe two different aspects of the experiment: the
speed, measured by the dimensionless Froude number
$Fn=v/\sqrt{gL}\in[0.125,0.45]$, and the hull geometry, summarized by five form
factors: \texttt{LCB}, \texttt{prismatic}, \texttt{L\_disp},
\texttt{beam\_draught} and \texttt{L\_beam} (\cref{tab:variables}). The hull
variables are constant within a hull, whereas the Froude number changes across
the towing-tank runs.

The scientific question is motivated by hydrodynamic theory. For sailing yachts,
residuary resistance is expected to follow an approximate power law in the
Froude number,
\[
	\mathrm{resistance}\propto Fn^{\gamma_F},
\]
with exponent close to $4$ in the bulk regime. The project asks whether this
relationship is visible in the data, which hull-geometry variables still matter
after accounting for speed, and how the inference changes once the clear
heteroscedasticity in resistance is modelled directly.

We address these questions in a Bayesian framework:
\begin{enumerate}
	\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
	\item log--log regression to estimate and \emph{verify} $\hat\beta_F\approx4$, with a prior-sensitivity check;
	\item BAS/BMA over the $2^5$ hull-factor submodels, to see which carry signal once $Fn$ is included;
	\item a heteroscedasticity-corrected nonlinear model on the retained factors, contrasted with the log--log fit;
	\item posterior predictive curves, with and without the heteroscedasticity treatment, against the data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Log--log Regression}
\label{sec:loglog}

\subsection{Model and prior}

Taking logarithms turns the physical power law $\mathrm{resistance}\propto Fn^{\,\gamma_F}$ into a linear model in which the exponent appears directly as a regression slope. We therefore fit
\begin{equation}
	\log(\mathrm{resistance})
	= \beta_0 + \beta_F\,\log(\mathrm{Froude})
	+ \sum_{k=1}^{5}\beta_k\,\mathrm{hull}_k + \varepsilon,
	\qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
	\label{eq:loglog}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{hull}_k$ denotes the five geometric form factors; the smallest observed resistance is $0.01>0$, so the logarithm is well defined for every record and no offset is required. As a baseline we adopt the non-informative \textbf{Jeffreys reference prior}
\begin{equation}
	\pi(\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^2)\;\propto\;1,
	\qquad
	\pi(\sigma^2)\;\propto\;\sigma^{-2},
	\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
which lets the data dominate the inference; the sensitivity of the conclusions to an informative prior is examined in \cref{sec:informative}.

\subsection{Posterior and credible intervals}

With the Jeffreys reference prior in Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior}, the Gaussian linear model has a closed-form posterior. Let $\hat{\bm{\beta}}=(\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{y}$. Then
\begin{align}
	\bm{\beta}\mid \sigma^2,\bm{y} & \sim
	\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}},\,
	             \sigma^2\,(\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\right), \label{eq:post_beta}
\end{align}
Integrating out $\sigma^2$ gives a multivariate Student-$t$ posterior for $\bm{\beta}$ with $n-p$ degrees of freedom, so the $95\%$ credible intervals coincide numerically with the classical OLS confidence intervals.

\subsection{Results}

\Cref{tab:loglog} (in the appendix) reports the posterior summaries. The model explains the data very well ($R^2=0.97$, residual standard deviation $s=0.32$ on the log scale) and the posterior of the Froude exponent is sharply concentrated,
\[
	\beta_F \approx 4.69, \qquad
	95\%\ \text{CI} = [4.60,\ 4.78],
\]
confirming the expected power-law behaviour $\mathrm{resistance}\sim Fn^{\,4}$ and verifying the project requirement $\hat\beta_F\approx 4$.

\Cref{fig:loglog} (appendix) shows the log--log scatter with the posterior-mean line and its very tight $95\%$ credible band (left), and the posterior of $\beta_F$ (right): the whole posterior mass sits above the nominal value $4$ ($\Pr(\beta_F>4\mid\bm{y})\approx 1$), so the empirical exponent is best described as \emph{slightly steeper} than the textbook value of four.

\subsection{Sensitivity check: independent informative priors}
\label{sec:informative}

The reference-prior analysis \emph{verifies} $\beta_F\approx4$ a posteriori; a complementary check \emph{encodes} the physics in the prior and asks how strongly the prior must insist on $4$ before it overrides the data. We replace Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior} with \textbf{independent proper priors},
\begin{equation}
	\beta_F \sim \mathcal{N}(4,\tau^2),
	\qquad
	\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,100^2)\ \ (j\neq F),
	\qquad
	\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}(10^{-3},10^{-3}),
	\label{eq:inf_prior}
\end{equation}
all components mutually independent. Since $\bm{\beta}$ and $\sigma^2$ are now independent a priori, unlike in the conjugate Normal--Inverse-Gamma family, the joint posterior is no longer available in closed form, but both full conditionals remain standard,
\begin{align}
	\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2},\bm{y} & \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm{V}(\bm{S}_0^{-1}\bm{m}_0+\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{y}/\sigma^{2}),\ \bm{V}\big),
	\qquad \bm{V}=(\bm{S}_0^{-1}+\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X}/\sigma^{2})^{-1}, \label{eq:fc_beta}                                                                            \\
	\sigma^{2}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{y} & \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}\!\big(a_0+\tfrac{n}{2},\ b_0+\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\rVert^{2}\big), \label{eq:fc_sigma}
\end{align}
where $\bm{m}_0$ and $\bm{S}_0$ collect the prior means and variances of Eq.~\eqref{eq:inf_prior}. We therefore sample the posterior with a \textbf{Gibbs sampler} ($10\,000$ iterations, $2\,000$ burn-in; the effective sample size for $\beta_F$ exceeds $4\,000$ in every run) and sweep the prior strength $\tau\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5\}$.

\Cref{tab:informative} (appendix) summarises the sweep; the posterior densities are overlaid in \cref{fig:informative} (appendix). A moderately informative prior ($\tau=0.5$) reproduces the reference result. Tightening the prior pulls the posterior mean towards $4$, but only the near-dogmatic $\tau=0.05$ (prior $95\%$ interval $\approx[3.9,\,4.1]$) drags it as far as $4.33$, and even then $\Pr(\beta_F>4\mid\bm{y})=1$.

For any moderately diffuse physical prior ($\tau\ge0.1$) the likelihood dominates and the posterior mean stays in $4.56$--$4.68$. The conclusion that the empirical exponent is genuinely steeper than $Fn^{\,4}$ is therefore \textbf{robust to the prior}: it is a feature of the $n=308$ observations, not an artefact of the non-informative choice, a transparent instance of prior--data conflict, in which an informative likelihood overwhelms all but an unreasonably confident prior.

\section{BAS/BMA model selection over hull covariates}
\label{sec:bma}

\subsection{Model space and forced Froude effect}

The log--log regression in \cref{sec:loglog} establishes that the dominant
physical scaling is the Froude power law. The model-selection question is
therefore conditional on Froude: after $\log(\mathrm{Froude})$ has been
included, do any hull-geometry variables still carry independent information
about $\log(\mathrm{resistance})$? For this reason the term
$\log(\mathrm{Froude})$ is forced into every candidate model; the BAS/BMA step is
not intended to test whether speed matters, but to identify possible secondary
hull effects after speed has been accounted for.

The optional covariates are the five hull form factors \texttt{LCB},
\texttt{prismatic}, \texttt{L\_disp}, \texttt{beam\_draught} and
\texttt{L\_beam}. They are treated as optional because the full log--log
regression does not resolve any of them individually: their credible intervals
cross zero, and the form factors are strongly related across the limited set of
$22$ hull designs. With five optional variables, the model space contains only
$2^5=32$ submodels. We can therefore enumerate the whole space with
\texttt{bas.lm}, using \texttt{include.always = \textasciitilde{} log\_Fr} and a
uniform prior over the 32 hull-covariate submodels.

This averaging approach is preferable to interpreting only the full regression
coefficients. The full model conditions on all five hull covariates being
present simultaneously, even though they are correlated and weakly identified.
BAS/BMA instead averages over all possible subsets and reports, for each
covariate, its posterior inclusion probability (PIP): the posterior probability
that the covariate belongs in the regression model, conditional on
$\log(\mathrm{Froude})$ being included.

\subsection{Baseline BAS/BMA results}

The baseline analysis uses the BIC marginal-likelihood approximation in
\texttt{bas.lm} with a uniform model prior. The resulting PIPs are shown in
\cref{tab:bma_pip}. A large PIP would indicate that a covariate appears
repeatedly in high-posterior-probability models, while a small PIP means that
the data do not require that term once Froude and the competing hull variables
are considered.

agrees with the full-model coefficient intervals in \cref{tab:loglog}, but is
more defensible because it checks the whole model space rather than relying on a
single saturated regression.

\subsection{Sensitivity to prior choices}

Because the baseline PIPs are modest, the BAS/BMA conclusion was checked under
alternative BAS/BMA prior specifications. The same model space was enumerated in
each run: $\log(\mathrm{Froude})$ was forced into every model, the five hull
covariates were optional, and the model prior over the $2^5=32$ submodels was
kept uniform. The prior specifications compared were the baseline
\texttt{BIC / reference} approximation, Zellner's \texttt{g-prior (g = n)}, and
the heavy-tailed \texttt{JZS} prior. The resulting PIPs are reported in
\cref{tab:bas_sensitivity}.

The sensitivity analysis reinforces the need for caution. The robust conclusion
is not that \texttt{beam\_draught} is definitely selected. In fact, no hull
covariate reaches $\mathrm{PIP}\ge0.50$ under all three prior specifications,
and the magnitude of the inclusion probabilities changes noticeably. The robust
conclusion is instead that all hull covariates are much weaker than the forced
Froude effect once $\log(\mathrm{Froude})$ is included. At the same time,
\texttt{beam\_draught} remains the best-supported secondary covariate in the
baseline analysis and the top-ranked hull covariate in each sensitivity run, so
it remains a reasonable candidate rather than an arbitrary choice. The observed
prior sensitivity motivates a cautious interpretation; it does not invalidate
the analysis, but it prevents any strong claim that a hull covariate has been
unambiguously selected.

\subsection{Interpretation and link with the nonlinear model}

Overall, BAS/BMA suggests that \texttt{beam\_draught} is the most defensible
secondary covariate, while the Froude power law remains the dominant robust
signal. This gives a coherent reduced modelling strategy for the next section:
the heteroscedastic nonlinear analysis should compare a Froude-only model
$M_0$ with a model $M_1$ that also includes \texttt{beam\_draught}. In this
workflow, BAS/BMA proposes the candidate hull covariate, and the nonlinear
original-scale model checks whether that covariate improves posterior
predictive performance once the variance growth with resistance is modelled
explicitly.

\section{Heteroscedasticity-Corrected Nonlinear Model}


\subsection{Nonlinear model}

Following the BAS/BMA analysis, the only hull-form variable carried forward was the beam-draught ratio, whose posterior inclusion probability was about $0.565$, so the evidence was moderate rather than strong.
Therefore, we fitted both a Froude-only model \textbf{M0} and a model including beam-draught \textbf{M1}. Because the residuary resistance is strictly positive, the main heteroscedastic nonlinear model uses a \textbf{Gamma} likelihood, parametrized through its mean and variance,
\[
Y_i \mid \theta,\sigma_0,\delta
\sim
\mathrm{Gamma}\!\Big(\text{shape}_i=\tfrac{\mu_i^2}{\sigma_i^2},\ \text{rate}_i=\tfrac{\mu_i}{\sigma_i^2}\Big),
\qquad
\mathbb{E}(Y_i)=\mu_i,\quad \mathrm{Var}(Y_i)=\sigma_i^2,
\]
where $Y_i$ is the observed residuary resistance and
\[
\mu_i
=
k\,\mathrm{Froude}_i^{\gamma_F}
\exp\{\beta_B\,\mathrm{bd}_i\}.
\]
Here $\mathrm{bd}_i$ is the beam--draught ratio (\texttt{beam\_draught}), and
$k>0$ is the multiplicative constant of the power law.
To model heteroscedasticity, we did not assume a constant variance. Instead, we used
\[
\sigma_i = \sigma_0 \mu_i^\delta .
\]
The parameter $\delta$ controls how fast the standard deviation grows with the fitted mean.
If $\delta=0$, then the variance is constant.
If $\delta=1$, then the standard deviation is proportional to the mean (constant coefficient of variation), which is close to the multiplicative-error interpretation of the log-log model.

The parameters to be estimated are the constant $k$, the Froude exponent $\gamma_F$, the
hull coefficient $\beta_B$, the baseline noise level $\sigma_0$, and the
\textbf{variance-growth exponent} $\delta$. We also test the heteroscedasticity correction
by comparing the fixed-variance version ($\delta=0$) with the learned-variance version using WAIC and PSIS-LOO.

For computation, the mean was rewritten in exponential form,
\[
\mu_i
=
\exp\{\log k+\gamma_F \log(\mathrm{Froude}_i)+\beta_B \mathrm{bd}_i\},
\]
so that it is a log-linear predictor in the parameters, and the Gamma shape and rate are obtained
from $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ by moment matching as above.




\subsection{Prior distributions}
\label{sec:t3-prior}

All priors are weakly informative and mutually independent. For the mean
parameters,
\begin{equation}
  \log k \sim \mathcal{N}(0,10^2),
  \qquad
  \gamma_F \sim \mathcal{N}(4,2^2),
  \qquad
  \beta_B \sim \mathcal{N}(0,2^2).
  \label{eq:t3-prior-mean}
\end{equation}
The prior on $\gamma_F$ is centred at the physical value $4$. For the variance function,
\begin{equation}
  \log\sigma_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,10^2),
  \qquad
  \delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1^2).
  \label{eq:t3-prior-var}
\end{equation}
The prior on $\delta$ is centred at $0$, i.e.\ at homoscedasticity, precisely so
that any evidence of variance growth comes from the data and not from the prior.
As a sensitivity check on the headline quantity, the $\gamma_F$ prior is also
replaced by a vague $\mathcal{N}(0,10^2)$ and by an informative
$\mathcal{N}(4,0.5^2)$.

\subsection{Posterior sampling algorithm}
\label{sec:t3-algorithm}

Unlike the log-log linear model, the posterior distribution is not available in closed form.
The likelihood contains a nonlinear exponential mean, and the variance also depends on the mean through $\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta$. Because of this non-conjugacy, we used MCMC in JAGS.

For this model JAGS automatically assigned the univariate slice sampler to the continuous parameters.

We run four independent chains, started from dispersed values of $\gamma_F$ ($3,6,5,4$) and of $\delta$ ($0,1,0.5,1.2$).
Each chain uses an adaptation phase, a burn-in that is discarded, and a thinned sampling phase.
If chains launched from very different points all settle on the same
distribution, that is good evidence the sampler has reached the posterior rather
than a starting-value-dependent region.

\subsection{Convergence diagnostics}
\label{sec:t3-convergence}

Before doing any interpretation from the posterior distribution or sampling from the posterior, we should check whether our proposed model has converged.
We checked the convergence of the nonlinear model with several different methods. First we check the Gelman--Rubin statistic $\hat{R}$. This compares the variance
between the dispersed chains to the variance within each chain. If the chains have all converged to the same distribution the two variances agree.
Secondly, we check Effective sample size (ESS). Successive MCMC draws are autocorrelated, so $N$ kept draws carry less information than $N$ independent ones. The ESS estimates the equivalent number of independent draws; we want it in the thousands so that posterior means and quantiles are estimated with little Monte-Carlo error.
Trace plots, for each parameter the four chains should look like
        stationary noise fluctuating around a common, fixed level, with the
        chains overlapping rather than drifting or sitting at separate values.
Finally, Autocorrelation function (ACF), For each parameter the autocorrelation of the chain should decay to zero within a few lags.

The Froude exponent is estimated at $\gamma_F\approx4.82$, with a $95\%$ credible
interval $[4.70,4.95]$ that lies entirely above the physical value $4$
($\Pr(\gamma_F>4\mid\bm{y})\approx1$). This is very close to the log-log estimate
$\beta_F\approx4.69$ of \cref{sec:loglog}, so the original-scale Gamma model and
the log-log model agree on the power-law exponent. The variance-growth exponent
is $\delta\approx0.89$, credibly positive ($\Pr(\delta>0\mid\bm{y})\approx1$) and
close to $1$, which means the standard deviation grows almost in proportion to the
mean. The data are therefore clearly heteroscedastic, and the variance grows
almost as fast as the multiplicative assumption that is implicit in the log-log
model.

The hull covariate is weak under this model. The beam-draught coefficient is
$\beta_B\approx0.05$, but its $95\%$ credible interval $[-0.013,0.113]$ contains
zero ($\Pr(\beta_B<0\mid\bm{y})\approx0.06$), so it is not credibly different from
zero. In other words, once the positive-support Gamma likelihood is used, the
moderate BAS evidence for \texttt{beam\_draught} does not become a credible
physical effect.

We also performed a prior sensitivity check for $\gamma_F$ to test the proposed physical value 4. The main prior is $\gamma_F\sim\mathcal N(4,2^2)$. To see whether the posterior estimate was mainly driven by this prior, we repeated the analysis with a vague prior, $\mathcal N(0,10^2)$, and with a more informative prior, $\mathcal N(4,0.5^2)$. The posterior mean was about $4.82$ under the vague prior and about $4.80$ under the informative prior, essentially unchanged from the main prior. This shows that the conclusion $\gamma_F\approx4.8$ is driven by the likelihood and the data, not by the prior.

\subsection{Model comparison}
\label{sec:t3-modelcomp}

We have three checks to validate the modelling choices, all answered with
WAIC and PSIS-LOO computed from the pointwise log-likelihood
$\log p(y_i\mid\bm{\theta}^{(s)})$ of the MCMC draws (lower WAIC/LOOIC is
better; PSIS-LOO additionally returns Pareto-$\hat k$ diagnostics that warn of
overly influential observations).\\

\paragraph{Check 1 --- covariate selection (was the BAS analysis correct?).}
We compare the Froude-only mean structure \textbf{M0} against \textbf{M1}, which
adds the BAS-selected hull covariate \texttt{beam\_draught} (both with the learned
variance and the Gamma likelihood). Under the Gamma likelihood the two models are
essentially tied (\cref{tab:t3_mean_compare}): WAIC improves by only $0.3$, and the
LOO difference, $\Delta\mathrm{elpd}=-0.2\pm1.9$, is well within one standard
error. So \texttt{beam\_draught} barely earns its place. This agrees with the
posterior summary, where $\beta_B$ is not credibly different from zero. We keep
M1 as the reference model to stay aligned with the BAS selection of
\cref{sec:bma}, but the hull-geometry covariate adds little beyond the Froude
power law.

\paragraph{Check 3 --- likelihood: Gamma vs.\ additive Normal.} The Gamma keeps
the response on its positive support. As a likelihood-sensitivity check we refit
the identical M1 mean $\mu_i=\exp(\bm{x}_i^\top\bm{\theta})$ and the identical
learned scale $\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^{\delta}$ under an additive Normal
likelihood, $Y_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)$, which is the most literal
reading of the additive error in the project formula. The priors and the free
$\delta$ are identical, so the likelihood family is the only thing that changes,
and because both models are densities of the same observable on the same original
scale their WAIC/PSIS-LOO are directly comparable.

\Cref{tab:t3_lik_compare} reports the result. On fit the two are a tie: WAIC
differs by only about $2$, and $\Delta\mathrm{elpd}=-1.1$ against
$\mathrm{se}_{\Delta}=16.9$, well within Monte-Carlo noise; all Pareto-$\hat
k<0.6$, so this null result is reliable. The data therefore cannot prefer one
likelihood over the other on fit alone. They differ in two other ways. First, the
additive Normal places about $13\%$ of its posterior-predictive mass below zero,
which is impossible for a resistance, while the Gamma places none; the Normal does
give sharper bands (mean 95\% width $5.5$ vs.\ $9.7$), but partly through this
negative tail. Second, and more important, the inference itself changes: the
additive Normal inflates the Froude exponent to $\gamma_F\approx6.4$ and forces
\texttt{beam\_draught} credibly negative, neither of which survives under the
Gamma. We keep the Gamma as the main model, both because it respects the support
and because its $\gamma_F\approx4.8$ agrees with the independent log-log estimate,
and we read the additive Normal as evidence that the steep exponent and the
negative hull effect are artifacts of the error model rather than robust physical
effects.





\section{Posterior Predictive Curves}
\label{sec:posterior_predictive}

The final comparison is made on the original resistance scale, because this is
where the data are observed and where the two error assumptions imply visibly
different uncertainty bands. For a fair comparison, both curves in
\cref{fig:posterior_predictive_curves} are posterior predictive curves: each
posterior draw includes uncertainty in the mean curve and one new
observation-level error term. The observed yacht-resistance data are overlaid on
the same scale.

For the log--log regression, the posterior predictive distribution is lognormal
after transforming back to resistance. After drawing $(\bm{\beta},\sigma^2)$ from
the posterior, replicated values are generated as
\[
	\log y_i^{\mathrm{rep}} \sim
	\mathcal{N}(x_i^\top\bm{\beta},\sigma^2),
	\qquad
	y_i^{\mathrm{rep}}=\exp(\log y_i^{\mathrm{rep}}).
\]
For the heteroscedastic nonlinear M1 model, replicated values are generated on
the original scale from the Gamma likelihood used in Task 3. The distribution is
parameterized by a mean-dependent standard deviation,
\[
	y_i^{\mathrm{rep}} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_i,r_i),
	\qquad
	\mu_i=\exp\{b_0+\gamma_F\widetilde{\log Fn}_i+
	\beta_B\widetilde{bd}_i\},
	\qquad
	\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta .
\]
Here $\alpha_i=(\mu_i/\sigma_i)^2$ and $r_i=\mu_i/\sigma_i^2$, so the
posterior predictive distribution has mean $\mu_i$, standard deviation
$\sigma_i$, and positive support.
The displayed curves hold the hull form fixed at a representative value:
\texttt{beam\_draught} is set to its sample median, while the other log--log
hull covariates are set to their sample means. This isolates the
Froude--resistance relationship instead of mixing different hull geometries.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/posterior_predictive_curves.pdf}
	\caption{Posterior predictive curves on the original resistance scale. The
		blue band is the log--log model after back-transformation, and the red band
		is the heteroscedastic nonlinear M1 model. Points are the observed yacht
		resistance data.}
	\label{fig:posterior_predictive_curves}
\end{figure}

The same posterior predictive simulations can be summarized over the original
308 observations by checking empirical $95\%$ coverage and the average width of
the predictive bands. These quantities are reported in \cref{tab:ppc_coverage}.
The log--log model gives the widest intervals and slightly over-covers the data.
The constant-variance Gamma M1 comparison is much too narrow, covering only
$67.9\%$ of the observations. Allowing the Gamma M1 standard deviation to grow as
$\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta$ raises coverage to $93.2\%$ while keeping the
average band width less than half of the log--log band width.

		$95\%$ posterior predictive interval.}
	\label{tab:ppc_coverage}
	\begin{tabular}{lrr}
		\toprule
		Model & Coverage & Mean band width \\
		\midrule
		Log--log, multiplicative errors & 0.968 & 12.241 \\

	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/posterior_predictive_widths.pdf}
	\caption{Width of the pointwise $95\%$ posterior predictive bands along the
		Froude-number grid used in \cref{fig:posterior_predictive_curves}. The
		constant-variance Gamma M1 comparison stays too narrow at high resistance,
		while the learned-variance Gamma M1 band expands with the mean response.}
	\label{fig:posterior_predictive_widths}
\end{figure}

The log--log fit produces a lognormal predictive distribution after
back-transformation. Its band is positive by construction, tight near zero
resistance, and expands approximately multiplicatively as resistance grows. The
Gamma M1 fit has the same positive-support property, but the variance growth is
estimated directly on the original resistance scale. In the fitted model
$\gamma_F\approx4.82$ and $\delta\approx0.89$, so the mean curve and uncertainty
growth are close to the log--log result rather than dramatically different from
it. The width comparison in \cref{fig:posterior_predictive_widths} makes the
heteroscedasticity effect explicit: the learned-variance Gamma model expands the
predictive band where resistance is large, unlike the constant-variance
comparison.

The important difference is interpretability on the original scale. The Gamma
model learns $\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta$ instead of imposing a constant
absolute residual scale, and the constant-variance comparison in Task 3 was
strongly rejected. At the same time, using the positive-support Gamma likelihood
removes the impossible negative predictive tail of the additive-Normal
sensitivity fit. Thus the posterior predictive curves support the updated Task 3
conclusion: heteroscedasticity must be modelled, the Froude power law is stable,
and \texttt{beam\_draught} is not a robustly resolved hull effect under the main
likelihood.

\section{Train/Test Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

As an additional predictive check, we also split the data randomly into training
and test sets, using about $80\%$ of the observations for training and $20\%$ for
testing. This validation is not used to replace the full-data Bayesian analysis:
the scientific conclusions above are based on the posterior fitted to all 308
observations. The split is only a held-out check of whether the posterior
predictive distributions behave sensibly on unseen rows.

The validation is kept on the same Bayesian scale as the posterior predictive
curves. Instead of comparing only point predictions through RMSE or MAE, each
candidate model is evaluated through its posterior predictive distribution on
the test rows. For the log--log model, the predictive density is lognormal on the
original resistance scale. For the nonlinear models, the predictive density is
Gamma on the original scale, either with constant residual variance or with the
learned heteroscedastic variance $\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta$.

For each test observation we compute the posterior predictive log density,
\[
	\log p(y_i^{\mathrm{test}}\mid y^{\mathrm{train}}),
\]
and then summarize the models by total log predictive density, mean log
predictive density, $95\%$ predictive-interval coverage, and average predictive
band width. Higher log predictive density indicates better held-out predictive
accuracy. Coverage close to $95\%$ indicates calibrated uncertainty, while the
band width checks whether good coverage is achieved efficiently rather than by
making intervals unnecessarily wide.

learned-variance Gamma M1 model corrects most of that under-coverage, reaching
$93.5\%$ test coverage with a moderate mean band width of 6.055. The log--log
model has the best held-out log predictive density and the highest coverage, but
it does so with substantially wider intervals. Thus the validation checks both
the location of the fitted curve and the uncertainty around it, confirming that
the heteroscedasticity treatment improves calibration without reducing the
comparison to a non-Bayesian point-fit exercise.

\paragraph{Hull-geometry covariates.}
Once the Froude number is in the model, none of the five hull form factors is individually credible: every $95\%$ interval comfortably contains zero (\cref{tab:loglog} and \cref{fig:forest}). The form factors vary only across the $22$ hulls and are strongly collinear --- the \texttt{prismatic} coefficient, for instance, has a posterior standard deviation of about $1.6$. This near-redundancy is precisely what the BAS/BMA model selection in \cref{sec:bma} resolves, through posterior inclusion probabilities rather than single-model $p$-values.

\paragraph{Residual diagnostics.}
Although the global fit is excellent, the residuals (\cref{fig:resid}) reveal two systematic defects. First, a $\cup$-shaped trend against the fitted values: a single constant exponent slightly over-predicts at intermediate speeds and under-predicts at the highest speeds. Both findings directly motivate the heteroscedasticity-corrected nonlinear model developed in the main text.
