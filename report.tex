\section{Data and Problem}
\label{sec:intro}

We analyse the \emph{Yacht Hydrodynamics} dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository \autocite{ortigosa2007yacht,gerritsma1981delft}: $22$ sailing-yacht hull forms tested at several speeds, for $n=308$ observations. Each row holds five hull \emph{form factors} --- \texttt{LCB}, \texttt{prismatic}, \texttt{L\_disp}, \texttt{beam\_draught} and \texttt{L\_beam} (constant within a hull) --- and the dimensionless \textbf{Froude number} $Fn=v/\sqrt{gL}\in[0.125,0.45]$; the response is the residuary resistance per unit weight of displacement (\cref{tab:variables}).

For slender hulls, physics predicts a power law $\mathrm{resistance}\propto Fn^{\,\gamma_F}$ with $\gamma_F\approx4$. We study it in a Bayesian framework:
\begin{enumerate}
	\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
	\item log--log regression to estimate and \emph{verify} $\hat\beta_F\approx4$, with a prior-sensitivity check;
	\item BAS/BMA over the $2^5$ hull-factor submodels, to see which carry signal once $Fn$ is included;
	\item a heteroscedasticity-corrected nonlinear model on the retained factors, contrasted with the log--log fit;
	\item posterior predictive curves, with and without the heteroscedasticity treatment, against the data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Log--log Regression}
\label{sec:loglog}

\subsection{Model and prior}

Taking logarithms turns the physical power law $\mathrm{resistance}\propto Fn^{\,\gamma_F}$ into a linear model in which the exponent appears directly as a regression slope. We therefore fit
\begin{equation}
	\log(\mathrm{resistance})
	= \beta_0 + \beta_F\,\log(\mathrm{Froude})
	+ \sum_{k=1}^{5}\beta_k\,\mathrm{hull}_k + \varepsilon,
	\qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
	\label{eq:loglog}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{hull}_k$ denotes the five geometric form factors; the smallest observed resistance is $0.01>0$, so the logarithm is well defined for every record and no offset is required. As a baseline we adopt the non-informative \textbf{Jeffreys reference prior}
\begin{equation}
	\pi(\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^2)\;\propto\;1,
	\qquad
	\pi(\sigma^2)\;\propto\;\sigma^{-2},
	\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
which lets the data dominate the inference; the sensitivity of the conclusions to an informative prior is examined in \cref{sec:informative}.

\subsection{Posterior and credible intervals}

With the Jeffreys reference prior in Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior}, the Gaussian linear model has a closed-form posterior. Let $\hat{\bm{\beta}}=(\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{y}$. Then
\begin{align}
	\bm{\beta}\mid \sigma^2,\bm{y} & \sim
	\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}},\,
	             \sigma^2\,(\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\right), \label{eq:post_beta}
\end{align}
Integrating out $\sigma^2$ gives a multivariate Student-$t$ posterior for $\bm{\beta}$ with $n-p$ degrees of freedom, so the $95\%$ credible intervals coincide numerically with the classical OLS confidence intervals.

\subsection{Results}

\Cref{tab:loglog} (in the appendix) reports the posterior summaries. The model explains the data very well ($R^2=0.97$, residual standard deviation $s=0.32$ on the log scale) and the posterior of the Froude exponent is sharply concentrated,
\[
	\beta_F \approx 4.69, \qquad
	95\%\ \text{CI} = [4.60,\ 4.78],
\]
confirming the expected power-law behaviour $\mathrm{resistance}\sim Fn^{\,4}$ and verifying the project requirement $\hat\beta_F\approx 4$.

\Cref{fig:loglog} (appendix) shows the log--log scatter with the posterior-mean line and its very tight $95\%$ credible band (left), and the posterior of $\beta_F$ (right): the whole posterior mass sits above the nominal value $4$ ($\Pr(\beta_F>4\mid\bm{y})\approx 1$), so the empirical exponent is best described as \emph{slightly steeper} than the textbook value of four.

\subsection{Sensitivity check: independent informative priors}
\label{sec:informative}

The reference-prior analysis \emph{verifies} $\beta_F\approx4$ a posteriori; a complementary check \emph{encodes} the physics in the prior and asks how strongly the prior must insist on $4$ before it overrides the data. We replace Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior} with \textbf{independent proper priors},
\begin{equation}
	\beta_F \sim \mathcal{N}(4,\tau^2),
	\qquad
	\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,100^2)\ \ (j\neq F),
	\qquad
	\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}(10^{-3},10^{-3}),
	\label{eq:inf_prior}
\end{equation}
all components mutually independent. Since $\bm{\beta}$ and $\sigma^2$ are now independent a priori, unlike in the conjugate Normal--Inverse-Gamma family, the joint posterior is no longer available in closed form, but both full conditionals remain standard,
\begin{align}
	\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2},\bm{y} & \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm{V}(\bm{S}_0^{-1}\bm{m}_0+\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{y}/\sigma^{2}),\ \bm{V}\big),
	\qquad \bm{V}=(\bm{S}_0^{-1}+\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X}/\sigma^{2})^{-1}, \label{eq:fc_beta}                                                                            \\
	\sigma^{2}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{y} & \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}\!\big(a_0+\tfrac{n}{2},\ b_0+\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\rVert^{2}\big), \label{eq:fc_sigma}
\end{align}
where $\bm{m}_0$ and $\bm{S}_0$ collect the prior means and variances of Eq.~\eqref{eq:inf_prior}. We therefore sample the posterior with a \textbf{Gibbs sampler} ($10\,000$ iterations, $2\,000$ burn-in; the effective sample size for $\beta_F$ exceeds $4\,000$ in every run) and sweep the prior strength $\tau\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5\}$.

\Cref{tab:informative} (appendix) summarises the sweep; the posterior densities are overlaid in \cref{fig:informative} (appendix). A moderately informative prior ($\tau=0.5$) reproduces the reference result. Tightening the prior pulls the posterior mean towards $4$, but only the near-dogmatic $\tau=0.05$ (prior $95\%$ interval $\approx[3.9,\,4.1]$) drags it as far as $4.33$, and even then $\Pr(\beta_F>4\mid\bm{y})=1$.

For any moderately diffuse physical prior ($\tau\ge0.1$) the likelihood dominates and the posterior mean stays in $4.56$--$4.68$. The conclusion that the empirical exponent is genuinely steeper than $Fn^{\,4}$ is therefore \textbf{robust to the prior}: it is a feature of the $n=308$ observations, not an artefact of the non-informative choice, a transparent instance of prior--data conflict, in which an informative likelihood overwhelms all but an unreasonably confident prior.

\section{BAS/BMA model selection over hull covariates}
\label{sec:bma}

The first part establishes the dominant physical relationship: after taking
logs, the resistance is essentially a power law of the Froude number. The
second question is conditional on that fact: once $\log(Fn)$ is already in the
model, which hull-geometry variables still carry independent signal?

We address this with Bayesian model averaging using \texttt{bas.lm}. The model
space contains the five optional geometry covariates
\texttt{LCB}, \texttt{prismatic}, \texttt{L\_disp}, \texttt{beam\_draught} and
\texttt{L\_beam}. Therefore the number of candidate submodels is only
$2^5=32$. The term $\log(Fn)$ is forced into every model, because the project is
not asking whether speed matters; it is asking which hull covariates matter
\emph{after} speed has been accounted for. In the R code this is enforced with
\texttt{include.always = \textasciitilde{} log\_Fr}, while the five hull terms
are left optional.

The main diagnostic is the posterior inclusion probability (PIP). A PIP is the
posterior probability that a covariate is included in the true regression model,
after averaging over all 32 submodels. Hence a large PIP indicates that the
variable repeatedly appears in high-posterior-probability models, while a small
PIP means that the data do not strongly need that variable once the other terms
are considered.

Using the BIC approximation to the marginal likelihood, the highest posterior
model is the one containing $\log(Fn)$ and \texttt{beam\_draught}, but its
posterior probability is not dominant. The approximate PIPs are reported in
\cref{tab:bma_pip}. The largest value is for \texttt{beam\_draught}, but even
there the evidence is only moderate. The remaining covariates have weak
inclusion probabilities. Thus the BAS/BMA conclusion is deliberately cautious:
the Froude power law is the stable signal; the hull-form variables may have
secondary effects, but no individual hull covariate is strongly identified by
the homoscedastic log--log regression.

This agrees with the full-model coefficient intervals in \cref{tab:loglog},
where all five hull coefficients have 95\% credible intervals crossing zero.
BAS/BMA strengthens that interpretation because it checks the whole model space
instead of relying on one full regression model.

\paragraph{Hull-geometry covariates.}
Once the Froude number is in the model, none of the five hull form factors is individually credible: every $95\%$ interval comfortably contains zero (\cref{tab:loglog} and \cref{fig:forest}). The form factors vary only across the $22$ hulls and are strongly collinear --- the \texttt{prismatic} coefficient, for instance, has a posterior standard deviation of about $1.6$. This near-redundancy is precisely what the BAS/BMA model selection in \cref{sec:bma} resolves, through posterior inclusion probabilities rather than single-model $p$-values.

\paragraph{Residual diagnostics.}
Although the global fit is excellent, the residuals (\cref{fig:resid}) reveal two systematic defects. First, a $\cup$-shaped trend against the fitted values: a single constant exponent slightly over-predicts at intermediate speeds and under-predicts at the highest speeds. Second, the residual spread is markedly larger at low fitted values, i.e.\ at low Froude numbers where the resistance is tiny and dominated by measurement noise --- a textbook signature of heteroscedasticity, also visible as the fanning of the data cloud on the left of \cref{fig:loglog}. Both findings directly motivate the heteroscedasticity-corrected nonlinear model developed in the main text.
