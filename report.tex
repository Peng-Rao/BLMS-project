\section{Data and Problem}
\label{sec:intro}

We analyse the \emph{Yacht Hydrodynamics} dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository \autocite{ortigosa2007yacht,gerritsma1981delft}: $22$ sailing-yacht hull forms tested at several speeds, for $n=308$ observations. Each row holds five hull \emph{form factors} --- \texttt{LCB}, \texttt{prismatic}, \texttt{L\_disp}, \texttt{beam\_draught} and \texttt{L\_beam} (constant within a hull) --- and the dimensionless \textbf{Froude number} $Fn=v/\sqrt{gL}\in[0.125,0.45]$; the response is the residuary resistance per unit weight of displacement (\cref{tab:variables}).

For slender hulls, physics predicts a power law $\mathrm{resistance}\propto Fn^{\,\gamma_F}$ with $\gamma_F\approx4$. We study it in a Bayesian framework:
\begin{enumerate}
	\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
	\item log--log regression to estimate and \emph{verify} $\hat\beta_F\approx4$, with a prior-sensitivity check;
	\item BAS/BMA over the $2^5$ hull-factor submodels, to see which carry signal once $Fn$ is included;
	\item a heteroscedasticity-corrected nonlinear model on the retained factors, contrasted with the log--log fit;
	\item posterior predictive curves, with and without the heteroscedasticity treatment, against the data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Log--log Regression}
\label{sec:loglog}

\subsection{Model and prior}

Taking logarithms turns the physical power law $\mathrm{resistance}\propto Fn^{\,\gamma_F}$ into a linear model in which the exponent appears directly as a regression slope. We therefore fit
\begin{equation}
	\log(\mathrm{resistance})
	= \beta_0 + \beta_F\,\log(\mathrm{Froude})
	+ \sum_{k=1}^{5}\beta_k\,\mathrm{hull}_k + \varepsilon,
	\qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
	\label{eq:loglog}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{hull}_k$ denotes the five geometric form factors; the smallest observed resistance is $0.01>0$, so the logarithm is well defined for every record and no offset is required. As a baseline we adopt the non-informative \textbf{Jeffreys reference prior}
\begin{equation}
	\pi(\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^2)\;\propto\;1,
	\qquad
	\pi(\sigma^2)\;\propto\;\sigma^{-2},
	\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
which lets the data dominate the inference; the sensitivity of the conclusions to an informative prior is examined in \cref{sec:informative}.

\subsection{Posterior and credible intervals}

With the Jeffreys reference prior in Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior}, the Gaussian linear model has a closed-form posterior. Let $\hat{\bm{\beta}}=(\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{y}$. Then
\begin{align}
	\bm{\beta}\mid \sigma^2,\bm{y} & \sim
	\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}},\,
	             \sigma^2\,(\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\right), \label{eq:post_beta}
\end{align}
Integrating out $\sigma^2$ gives a multivariate Student-$t$ posterior for $\bm{\beta}$ with $n-p$ degrees of freedom, so the $95\%$ credible intervals coincide numerically with the classical OLS confidence intervals.

\subsection{Results}

\Cref{tab:loglog} (in the appendix) reports the posterior summaries. The model explains the data very well ($R^2=0.97$, residual standard deviation $s=0.32$ on the log scale) and the posterior of the Froude exponent is sharply concentrated,
\[
	\beta_F \approx 4.69, \qquad
	95\%\ \text{CI} = [4.60,\ 4.78],
\]
confirming the expected power-law behaviour $\mathrm{resistance}\sim Fn^{\,4}$ and verifying the project requirement $\hat\beta_F\approx 4$.

\Cref{fig:loglog} (appendix) shows the log--log scatter with the posterior-mean line and its very tight $95\%$ credible band (left), and the posterior of $\beta_F$ (right): the whole posterior mass sits above the nominal value $4$ ($\Pr(\beta_F>4\mid\bm{y})\approx 1$), so the empirical exponent is best described as \emph{slightly steeper} than the textbook value of four.

\subsection{Sensitivity check: independent informative priors}
\label{sec:informative}

The reference-prior analysis \emph{verifies} $\beta_F\approx4$ a posteriori; a complementary check \emph{encodes} the physics in the prior and asks how strongly the prior must insist on $4$ before it overrides the data. We replace Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior} with \textbf{independent proper priors},
\begin{equation}
	\beta_F \sim \mathcal{N}(4,\tau^2),
	\qquad
	\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,100^2)\ \ (j\neq F),
	\qquad
	\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}(10^{-3},10^{-3}),
	\label{eq:inf_prior}
\end{equation}
all components mutually independent. Since $\bm{\beta}$ and $\sigma^2$ are now independent a priori, unlike in the conjugate Normal--Inverse-Gamma family, the joint posterior is no longer available in closed form, but both full conditionals remain standard,
\begin{align}
	\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2},\bm{y} & \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm{V}(\bm{S}_0^{-1}\bm{m}_0+\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{y}/\sigma^{2}),\ \bm{V}\big),
	\qquad \bm{V}=(\bm{S}_0^{-1}+\bm{X}^{\!\top}\bm{X}/\sigma^{2})^{-1}, \label{eq:fc_beta}                                                                            \\
	\sigma^{2}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{y} & \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}\!\big(a_0+\tfrac{n}{2},\ b_0+\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\rVert^{2}\big), \label{eq:fc_sigma}
\end{align}
where $\bm{m}_0$ and $\bm{S}_0$ collect the prior means and variances of Eq.~\eqref{eq:inf_prior}. We therefore sample the posterior with a \textbf{Gibbs sampler} ($10\,000$ iterations, $2\,000$ burn-in; the effective sample size for $\beta_F$ exceeds $4\,000$ in every run) and sweep the prior strength $\tau\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5\}$.

\Cref{tab:informative} (appendix) summarises the sweep; the posterior densities are overlaid in \cref{fig:informative} (appendix). A moderately informative prior ($\tau=0.5$) reproduces the reference result. Tightening the prior pulls the posterior mean towards $4$, but only the near-dogmatic $\tau=0.05$ (prior $95\%$ interval $\approx[3.9,\,4.1]$) drags it as far as $4.33$, and even then $\Pr(\beta_F>4\mid\bm{y})=1$.

For any moderately diffuse physical prior ($\tau\ge0.1$) the likelihood dominates and the posterior mean stays in $4.56$--$4.68$. The conclusion that the empirical exponent is genuinely steeper than $Fn^{\,4}$ is therefore \textbf{robust to the prior}: it is a feature of the $n=308$ observations, not an artefact of the non-informative choice, a transparent instance of prior--data conflict, in which an informative likelihood overwhelms all but an unreasonably confident prior.

\section{BAS/BMA model selection over hull covariates}
\label{sec:bma}

The first part establishes the dominant physical relationship: after taking
logs, the resistance is essentially a power law of the Froude number. The
second question is conditional on that fact: once $\log(Fn)$ is already in the
model, which hull-geometry variables still carry independent signal?

We address this with Bayesian model averaging using \texttt{bas.lm}. The model
space contains the five optional geometry covariates
\texttt{LCB}, \texttt{prismatic}, \texttt{L\_disp}, \texttt{beam\_draught} and
\texttt{L\_beam}. Therefore the number of candidate submodels is only
$2^5=32$. The term $\log(Fn)$ is forced into every model, because the project is
not asking whether speed matters; it is asking which hull covariates matter
\emph{after} speed has been accounted for. In the R code this is enforced with
\texttt{include.always = \textasciitilde{} log\_Fr}, while the five hull terms
are left optional.

The main diagnostic is the posterior inclusion probability (PIP). A PIP is the
posterior probability that a covariate is included in the true regression model,
after averaging over all 32 submodels. Hence a large PIP indicates that the
variable repeatedly appears in high-posterior-probability models, while a small
PIP means that the data do not strongly need that variable once the other terms
are considered.

Using the BIC approximation to the marginal likelihood, the highest posterior
model is the one containing $\log(Fn)$ and \texttt{beam\_draught}, but its
posterior probability is not dominant. The approximate PIPs are reported in
\cref{tab:bma_pip}. The largest value is for \texttt{beam\_draught}, but even
there the evidence is only moderate. The remaining covariates have weak
inclusion probabilities. Thus the BAS/BMA conclusion is deliberately cautious:
the Froude power law is the stable signal; the hull-form variables may have
secondary effects, but no individual hull covariate is strongly identified by
the homoscedastic log--log regression.

This agrees with the full-model coefficient intervals in \cref{tab:loglog},
where all five hull coefficients have 95\% credible intervals crossing zero.
BAS/BMA strengthens that interpretation because it checks the whole model space
instead of relying on one full regression model.

\section{Heteroscedasticity-Corrected Nonlinear Model}


\subsection{Nonlinear model}

Following the BAS/BMA analysis, the only hull-form variable carried forward was the beam-draught ratio with posterior inclusion probability was about $0.565$, so the evidence was moderate rather than strong. 
Therefore, we fitted both a Froude-only model \textbf{M0} and a model including beam-draught \textbf{M1}, but the main heteroscedastic nonlinear model was
\[
Y_i \mid \theta,\sigma_0,\delta
\sim
N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2),
\]
where $Y_i$ is the observed residuary resistance and
\[
\mu_i
=
k\,\mathrm{Froude}_i^{\gamma_F}
\exp\{\beta_B\,\mathrm{bd}_i\}.
\]

where $\mathrm{bd}_i$ is the beam--draught ratio (\texttt{beam\_draught}), and
$k>0$ is the multiplicative constant of the power law. 
To model heteroscedasticity, we did not assume a constant variance. Instead, we used
\[
\sigma_i = \sigma_0 \mu_i^\delta .
\]
The parameter $\delta$ controls how fast the standard deviation grows with the fitted mean. 
If $\delta=0$, then the model reduces to a homoscedastic additive-error model on the original scale. 
If $\delta=1$, then the standard deviation is proportional to the mean, which is close to the multiplicative-error interpretation of the log-log model. 


The parameters to be estimated are the constant $k$, the Froude exponent $\gamma_F$, the
hull-coefficient $\beta_B$, the baseline noise level $\sigma_0$, and the
\textbf{variance-growth exponent} $\delta$. We have include the test of heteroscedasticity correction
by comparing the fixed version of the variance ($\delta=0$) and version with learned variance with WAIC and PSIS-LOO tests.

For computation, the mean was rewritten in exponential form:
\[
\mu_i
=
\exp\{\log k+\gamma_F \log(\mathrm{Froude}_i)+\beta_B \mathrm{bd}_i\}.
\]
In the implementation we centered $\log(\mathrm{Froude})$ and beam-draught before fitting the model. 
Writing
$\log\mathrm{Froude}_i = m_F + \tilde{x}_{F,i}$ and
$\mathrm{bd}_i = m_B + \tilde{x}_{B,i}$ and absorbing the constants into a single
intercept $b_0$,
\begin{equation}
  b_0 = \log k + \gamma_F m_F + \beta_B m_B,
  \label{eq:t3-b0}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mu_i
  = \exp\!\big\{b_0 + \gamma_F\,\tilde{x}_{F,i} + \beta_B\,\tilde{x}_{B,i}\big\}.
  \label{eq:t3-centred}
\end{equation}
Centring relabels the intercept but leaves the slopes $\gamma_F$ and $\beta_B$
untouched, so it does not change the inference on the quantities of physical
interest. After sampling, the physical constant is recovered draw by draw from
\begin{equation}
  k = \exp\!\big\{b_0 - \gamma_F m_F - \beta_B m_B\big\}.
  \label{eq:t3-k}
\end{equation}




\subsection{Prior distributions}
\label{sec:t3-prior}

All priors are weakly informative and mutually independent. For the mean
parameters,
\begin{equation}
  b_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,10^2),
  \qquad
  \gamma_F \sim \mathcal{N}(4,2^2),
  \qquad
  \beta_B \sim \mathcal{N}(0,2^2).
  \label{eq:t3-prior-mean}
\end{equation}
The prior on $\gamma_F$ is centred at the physical value $4$. For the variance function,
\begin{equation}
  \log\sigma_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,10^2),
  \qquad
  \delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1^2).
  \label{eq:t3-prior-var}
\end{equation}
The prior on $\delta$ is centred at $0$, i.e.\ at homoscedasticity, precisely so
that any evidence of variance growth comes from the data and not from the prior.
As a sensitivity check on the headline quantity, the $\gamma_F$ prior is also
replaced by a vague $\mathcal{N}(0,10^2)$ and by an informative
$\mathcal{N}(4,0.5^2)$.

\subsection{Posterior sampling algorithm}
\label{sec:t3-algorithm}

Unlike log-log linear model, the posterior distribution is not availiable in closed form.
The likelihood contains a nonlinear exponential mean, and the variance also depends on the mean through $\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta$. Because of this non-conjugacy, we used MCMC in JAGS.

For this model JAGS automatically assigned the univariate slice sampler to the continuous parameters.

We run four independent chains, started from dispersed values of $\gamma_F$ ( $3,6,5,4$) and of $\delta$ (values $0,1,0.5,1.2$)
Each chain uses an adaptation phase, a burn-in that is discarded, and a thinned sampling phase.
If chains launched from very different points all settle on the same
distribution, that is good evidence the sampler has reached the posterior rather
than a starting-value-dependent region.

\subsection{Convergence diagnostics}
\label{sec:t3-convergence}

Before doing any intrepretion from posterior distribution or sampling from posterior, we should check wheter our proposed model has converged.
We checked the converges of the non-linear with several different methods. First we check Gelman--Rubin statistic $\hat{R}$. This compares the variance
between the dispersed chains to the variance within each chain. If the chains have all converged to the same distribution the two variances agree.
Secondly, we check Effective sample size (ESS). Successive MCMC draws are autocorrelated, so $N$ kept draws carry less information than $N$ independent ones. The ESS estimates the equivalent number of independent draws; we want it in the thousands so that posterior means and quantiles are estimated with little Monte-Carlo error.
Trace plots, for each parameter the four chains should look like
        stationary noise fluctuating around a common, fixed level, with the
        chains overlapping rather than drifting or sitting at separate values.
Finally, Autocorrelation function (ACF), For each parameter the autocorrelation of the chain should decay to zero within a few lags.

\subsection{Posterior analysis}
\label{sec:t3-posterior}

\subsection{Model comparison}
\label{sec:t3-modelcomp}

We use two questions to validate the modelling choices, both answered with
WAIC and PSIS-LOO computed from the pointwise log-likelihood
$\log p(y_i\mid\bm{\theta}^{(s)})$ of the MCMC draws (lower WAIC/LOOIC is
better; PSIS-LOO additionally returns Pareto-$\hat k$ diagnostics that warn of
overly influential observations).\\

First question is that was the BAS analysis true?

Second question is was learned variance correct the heteroscedasticity.

\section{Posterior Predictive Curves}
\label{sec:posterior_predictive}

The final comparison is made on the original resistance scale, because this is
where the data are observed and where the two error assumptions imply visibly
different uncertainty bands. For a fair comparison, both curves in
\cref{fig:posterior_predictive_curves} are posterior predictive curves: each
posterior draw includes uncertainty in the mean curve and one new
observation-level error term. The observed yacht-resistance data are overlaid on
the same scale.

For the log--log regression, the posterior predictive distribution is lognormal
after transforming back to resistance. After drawing $(\bm{\beta},\sigma^2)$ from
the posterior, replicated values are generated as
\[
	\log y_i^{\mathrm{rep}} \sim
	\mathcal{N}(x_i^\top\bm{\beta},\sigma^2),
	\qquad
	y_i^{\mathrm{rep}}=\exp(\log y_i^{\mathrm{rep}}).
\]
For the heteroscedastic nonlinear M1 model, replicated values are generated on
the original scale with a mean-dependent standard deviation,
\[
	y_i^{\mathrm{rep}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2),
	\qquad
	\mu_i=\exp\{b_0+\gamma_F\widetilde{\log Fn}_i+
	\beta_B\widetilde{bd}_i\},
	\qquad
	\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta .
\]
The displayed curves hold the hull form fixed at a representative value:
\texttt{beam\_draught} is set to its sample median, while the other log--log
hull covariates are set to their sample means. This isolates the
Froude--resistance relationship instead of mixing different hull geometries.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/posterior_predictive_curves.pdf}
	\caption{Posterior predictive curves on the original resistance scale. The
		blue band is the log--log model after back-transformation, and the red band
		is the heteroscedastic nonlinear M1 model. Points are the observed yacht
		resistance data.}
	\label{fig:posterior_predictive_curves}
\end{figure}

The log--log fit keeps the Froude exponent close to the physical fourth-power
law and produces multiplicative uncertainty. Its band is tight near zero
resistance and expands as resistance grows, which describes the low and middle
Froude range reasonably well. However, the mean curve is too flat at the highest
Froude values, where the observed resistance rises faster than the fitted
$Fn^{4.69}$ relationship.

The heteroscedastic nonlinear fit behaves differently. Its mean curve is steeper
($\gamma_F\approx6.4$) and follows the high-Froude observations more closely.
Because its variance is learned through
$\sigma_i=\sigma_0\mu_i^\delta$ with $\delta\approx0.4$, the predictive band is
not forced to have constant width. It widens where resistance and scatter are
larger and tightens toward the low-Froude region. This is the practical payoff of
the heteroscedasticity treatment: the uncertainty band adapts to the data rather
than imposing a single residual scale across the whole Froude range.

Overall, the posterior predictive curves show that the two models differ mainly
in how they scale uncertainty with resistance. The log--log model gives a useful
positive predictive distribution, but it imposes multiplicative uncertainty and
misses part of the high-speed curvature. The heteroscedastic nonlinear model
learns the variance growth directly, tracks the observed data more closely, and
supports the conclusion that the treatment of heteroscedasticity is essential
for interpreting both the Froude exponent and the selected hull covariate
\texttt{beam\_draught}.

\paragraph{Hull-geometry covariates.}
Once the Froude number is in the model, none of the five hull form factors is individually credible: every $95\%$ interval comfortably contains zero (\cref{tab:loglog} and \cref{fig:forest}). The form factors vary only across the $22$ hulls and are strongly collinear --- the \texttt{prismatic} coefficient, for instance, has a posterior standard deviation of about $1.6$. This near-redundancy is precisely what the BAS/BMA model selection in \cref{sec:bma} resolves, through posterior inclusion probabilities rather than single-model $p$-values.

\paragraph{Residual diagnostics.}
Although the global fit is excellent, the residuals (\cref{fig:resid}) reveal two systematic defects. First, a $\cup$-shaped trend against the fitted values: a single constant exponent slightly over-predicts at intermediate speeds and under-predicts at the highest speeds. Second, the residual spread is markedly larger at low fitted values, i.e.\ at low Froude numbers where the resistance is tiny and dominated by measurement noise --- a textbook signature of heteroscedasticity, also visible as the fanning of the data cloud on the left of \cref{fig:loglog}. Both findings directly motivate the heteroscedasticity-corrected nonlinear model developed in the main text.
